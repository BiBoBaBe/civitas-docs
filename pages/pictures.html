The prose contributes the action. Everything else comes from the world.

Take a concrete failure. A scene puts her glasses on the coffee table, and three beats later the picture draws her wearing them, because the paragraph the picture was made from did not happen to mention glasses. An image generator asked to illustrate a paragraph draws whatever that paragraph mentions. If it does not mention the room, the room is invented.

Civitas composes a picture from the state instead, and asks the model only the question it can actually answer.

Two separate questions

After a reply, one pass reads the prose for what changed in the world. A second, entirely separate pass decides what a picture would show. They are separate because they are different questions, and the answers are better when each is asked on its own.

The second pass, call it the director, decides who is in frame, where the camera is, how close the shot is, the angle, one line about how the bodies are arranged, and where in the room it is happening. It is told in as many words that it does not decide what anybody looks like, and that anything it says about hair, skin, age, clothes or build is thrown away.

Each half used to fail in an opposite way, which is how the split was arrived at. Reading the prose gave one pose for one person and everybody else fell out of the picture, so a moment with two people in it came out as a standing portrait of one of them. A generic illustrator gets the moment right and then invents the people, because all it has is the prose.

Who is in the frame

Whoever the scene was about. Failing that, the last person you were dealing with, but only if they are still in the room, because a live session once drew a picture of somebody who was in another flat entirely.

A group picture takes everybody standing where you are, four at most. Four is measured rather than chosen: the canvas is divided into a vertical band per person, and below a certain width a band no longer holds a standing figure. Asked for five, the model drew four and merged the leftmost two. The person the turn was about is never the one dropped.

And you are never in your own frame when the camera is your own eyes, however firmly a model tries to put you there.

Where they are

The room comes from the world's place, looked up in a table of tags installed alongside the art. A kitchen is described as a kitchen with pots on the stove and warm lamps, because that is what that place is. The director may pick a spot inside it, since a bedroom at the end of the hall is not the same room as the workroom, whatever the flat is called.

What they are wearing, and what came off

This is the part that only works because the world is keeping state.

Everybody has a pinned look and a small wardrobe by situation: home, work, a night out, casual, the gym. Which one is chosen comes from what they are actually doing. Clothes are a fact about the day and not a roll of the dice, so the woman working the taps is in her work clothes every single time.

Where somebody is outranks what they are doing. A live failure: somebody in a locked gym studio came out in a denim skirt, because the words for the moment had stopped saying gym the second the workout stopped. A gym is a gym for as long as she is standing in it.

What the world watched somebody change into beats what their card would guess from the hour. And what has come off is remembered, turn to turn and across the rooms of a flat, so a picture no longer depends on the one turn that happened to mention it.

A removed item has to be removed twice: taken out of the description, and stated as something that must not appear. Leaving a tag out only makes it absent, and the reference face and the model's own habits will cheerfully put glasses back on somebody who always wears them.

Shoes are dropped from any frame too close to contain them. A shoe in a close frame does not stay out of the picture, it finds somewhere else to go. It once put a pair of shoes in somebody's hands.

Keeping the same face

Every person has one neutral head-and-shoulders reference image, made once. At render time it steers the face and hair while the prompt still controls the outfit, the pose, the room and the framing. That is the whole reason she is the same woman in every picture.

It is a head crop rather than a whole portrait, because the technique copies whatever is in the crop, props and all. Its influence is held in a narrow band, since turned up further it starts choosing the outfit and the room as well. And if the portrait and the tags disagree you get a blend, because the technique cannot resolve a contradiction. Fix one or the other.

In a group picture each person's reference is masked to their own band. Asking for three people in one description gets you three people wearing a shuffled mix of three wardrobes.

One seed per scene

Rather than a new random seed per picture, the seed comes from who, where and which day. This was measured over six consecutive beats drawn twice. With free seeds the room changed wall by wall, the floor changed colour, and in one frame a pair of leggings came out as shorts. With one seed the room, the light and the clothes stayed put while the poses still read as six different moments.

Change the room, the person or the day and the picture starts afresh. A picture you asked for yourself gets a free seed, because pressing draw again and getting the same frame back is not drawing.

The rule about who is in a picture

An image contains exactly the people it is meant to contain. Three layers enforce that, and the third is the interesting one.

The description says so, and the canvas is never much taller than the subject, because a tall canvas is where a model stacks a second torso. A standing list of things that must not appear covers every phrasing that invites a bystander into frame. And then a detector runs after the picture is made, on its own graphics worker, counting faces, bodies and where it can, poses. Too many bodies is a stranger. Too few is usually two people merged. One face short is a body with no head on it, which is the failure nobody ships.

A frame that fails is deleted and drawn again with a new seed, three times, and then it fails closed. A wrong body count means no picture rather than a wrong picture.

Even the checks have a known hole, and it is written down rather than papered over: a second torso fused to the first gives the counter nothing to count, and is defended only by the description.

Pictures are best-effort

No graphics card, no pictures, and nothing else breaks. Only one picture is drawn per conversation at a time and the queue holds exactly one waiting job, because by the time the card is free the moment worth painting is the latest one and not the four before it. During a fast exchange, most turns simply get no picture.

Rooms are painted once, in advance

Every place in Tremont is painted twice, offline, and kept as files: an isometric cutaway like a dollhouse, and an eye-level backdrop with an empty band of floor along the bottom. Each exists at three times of day, and the day and dusk versions are relit from the night one rather than redrawn, so the geometry stays put and only the light changes.

The isometric ones illustrate this site. Two lessons from making them are worth passing on. Describe a room wall by wall rather than as a mood. And describe absent people positively, as in "an empty bartender's aisle with nobody in it", which is what finally stopped a stray bartender turning up in most of the frames.

Two kinds of picture

A character's portrait is one drawing, made once. A picture drawn in play is made fresh from their pinned look, what they would be wearing for what they are doing, and the room they are standing in. They are not meant to match, which is why every one drawn in play is captioned with who, where and when, and marked as drawn by the world.
