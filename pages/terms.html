A plan is a promise about one hour. A term is a promise about how somebody behaves for as long as it stands. The world holds the exact words, quotes them back to the narrator every turn, and refuses to let anybody quietly reword them.

Why the world holds them

People agree to things. Written by a narrator into prose, an agreement lasts exactly as long as the reply that carried it. Held by the world, it is said back until it lapses, and the world knows when a clause was not kept.

Terms cover a truce, a condition on a loan, an agreement between colleagues about what does not leave the room, a raid party's rules before a fight, and the more intimate kinds of arrangement. The engine has no taxonomy of clauses. A clause is a sentence somebody said.

What a term looks like

Clauses are separated by semicolons, because that is how somebody lists rules out loud and a comma lives inside half of them. A clause is at most a line, and a term has at most eight. Each can be attributed to one party or to neither.

<<term person="NAME" what="nobody goes past the second pillar alone; the tank calls the retreat; loot is split before we go in; safeword fall back" until="tonight" how="propose">>

A clause beginning "safeword" becomes the way out. The how field is propose, sign, counter, break or void, so the same tag carries a term through its whole life. A duration is read the way somebody would say one: an hour, tonight, a month, standing. Tonight ends at six in the morning. A length the world cannot read is refused. A term with an hour on it lapses by itself when the hour passes.

Signing

A term is proposed until everybody named has signed it, and proposing counts as signing for the person who proposed. While it is still on the table either side can redraft it, and redrafting wipes the signatures, because whoever changed the words is the only one who has agreed to them.

Once in force the words are frozen. A narrator that tries to adjust what somebody put their name to is refused:

that is already signed.
void it and draw up new terms instead of rewriting what somebody agreed to

Your own signature needs your own message. A narrator writing you agreeing is rejected outright.

Breaking one

Three things happen when a clause is broken.

  1. The term remembers it.
  2. The other parties think less of whoever did it, noticeably more than for being stood up, because breaking something you put your name to is a statement about you. Nothing is fatal on its own. Three broken clauses is.
  3. A fact is written that rumour can carry. The fact is about the breaking, not the clause, because somebody breaking their word is a story and the clause itself may be nobody's business.

What does not happen is the term ending. Ending it is the other party's move, and taking that away from them would be the world deciding how they feel about it.

The safeword

A safeword is held separately from the clauses, because it is not a rule about behaviour but the way out, and it has to be readable as one wherever the term is shown. The narrator is given it in full every turn, spelled out: the moment anybody says it, all of this is over. A narrator that has not been told the word cannot honour it.

Saying it ends the term immediately. It is not a breach. It writes no fact, moves no feelings and costs the person who used it nothing. The only thing that changes is the label in the log, because "she said the word" and "she called it off" are different evenings.

Underneath the safeword there is a plainer door: either party can end any term at any moment, for any reason or none, at no cost.

Couples

A couple is one party with two halves, and the halves do not want the same thing. The world holds who they are, a line about them, what each of them separately wants, which of them decides, and what is off the table. Without this, a narrator that does not know two people are a unit writes one of them as a stranger at a bar.

Limits are printed to the narrator last and as an instruction, because a limit buried in the middle of a sentence is a limit a model averages away.

Three yeses

When a third person is involved, one rule carries the feature: an invitation is three separate yeses, and any one no ends it.

Whoever raised it has said yes by raising it, and everybody else starts at not answered. One no closes it there and then, and a half who has said no is never asked again by the machinery. A new invitation clears the previous answers, because a yes in October is not a yes in November.

Your yes requires your own message. Even a fully agreed invitation is only an agreement, and the narrator is told so: it is agreed; it has not happened yet.

Afterwards, they may disagree

Each half can say separately what happened. The world does not adjudicate, because it does not know which of them is right and neither do they. If the two accounts differ, both pay a small amount of warmth and trust, in both directions. Nobody is lying. They had different evenings. The narrator gets both versions with one line attached: neither of them is lying.
