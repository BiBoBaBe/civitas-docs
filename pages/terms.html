A plan is a promise about one hour. A term is a promise about how somebody behaves, for as long as it stands.

The arrangement

A married couple, both lawyers. Four clauses, signed by both of them years ago, still standing. She says when, and she says who. He watches; he does not join in; he does not speak. Nobody either of them will have to meet again. And the fourth: the man does not fall in love with her.

That fourth clause is about somebody neither of them has met yet, which is exactly why it is written down. If you turn out to be that man, you are inside an agreement you did not sign, and the world is keeping score of a clause you cannot break without knowing you broke it.

Why the world holds them

People agree to things. Written by a narrator into prose, those agreements last exactly as long as the reply that carried them. Held here, the world goes on saying them back until they lapse, and it knows when one of them was not kept.

Terms are about consent, power, and what it costs to break your word. That covers a truce, a condition attached to a loan, an agreement between colleagues about what does not leave the room, and the more intimate kinds of arrangement. The engine has no taxonomy of clauses. A clause is a sentence somebody said, and the world's job is to remember it exactly.

What a term looks like

Clauses are separated by semicolons, because that is how somebody lists rules out loud and a comma lives inside half of them. A clause is a rule, not a paragraph: at most a line, at most eight of them. Each can be attributed to one party or to neither.

A duration is read the way somebody would say one: an hour, tonight, a month, standing. Tonight ends at six in the morning, because a night is until morning and not twenty-four hours. A length the world cannot read is refused rather than guessed at. A term with an hour on it lapses by itself when the hour passes, because "in force until 12:00" is a lie at 12:01.

Signing, and not being able to change it afterwards

A term is proposed until everybody named has signed it, and proposing counts as signing for the person who proposed. While it is still on the table either side can redraft it, and redrafting wipes the signatures, because whoever changed the words is the only one who has now agreed to them.

Once it is in force the words are frozen. This is the single thing the whole feature exists to make impossible. A narrator that tries to quietly adjust what somebody put their name to is refused, with a suggestion rather than an error: void it and draw up new terms instead of rewriting what somebody agreed to.

Breaking one

Three things happen, and the third is the interesting one. The term remembers it. The other parties think less of whoever did it, meaningfully more than for being stood up for a drink, because not turning up is a disappointment and breaking something you put your name to twenty minutes ago is a statement about you. Nothing is fatal on its own. Three broken clauses is.

And a fact is written that gossip can pick up like any other. The fact is about the breaking, not the clause, because somebody breaking their word is a story and the clause itself may be nobody's business.

What does not happen is the term ending. Ending it is the other party's move, and taking that away from them would be the world deciding how they feel about it.

The safeword

A safeword is lifted out of the list of clauses, because it is not a rule about behaviour, it is the way out, and it has to be readable as one wherever the term is shown. The narrator is given it in full, every time, spelled out: the moment anybody says it, all of this is over. A narrator that has not been told the word cannot honour it.

Saying it ends the term immediately. It is not a breach. It writes no fact, it moves no feelings, and it costs the person who used it nothing, because a safeword that costs trust is not a safeword. The only thing that changes is the label in the log and the recap, because "she said the word" and "she thought about it and called it off" are not the same evening.

Underneath the safeword there is a plainer door. Either party can end any term at any moment, for any reason or none, and that costs nothing either. It is the whole point of having it.

Couples

Half the cast of the example world arrived two by two, and the world held them as strangers who happened to share a postcode. That was expensive.

A narrator that does not know these two are a unit writes her as a woman at a bar.

A couple is one thing with two halves, and the halves do not want the same thing. Both are true at once, and the world has to be able to say so in one line. So a couple holds who they are, a line about them, what each of them separately wants, which of them decides, and what is off the table.

Two payments behind

A couple in the suburb are two payments behind on the smallest house on the street, and their arrangement is the only thing they still talk about properly. What he wants and what she wants are written down separately and they are not the same thing. She has told him, to his face, that he bores her, which is the cruellest and most useful sentence either of them owns. She decides. The limits are in their own words: not in their house, nobody he has to see at work, and she tells him first, every time.

Separately, and not in the couple record at all, she has been covering the shortfall on the house with a credit card he does not know exists, and his contract runs out in the new year and he has not told her. Two secrets, one mortgage, a normal Tuesday.

Limits are printed last and as an instruction, because a limit buried in the middle of a sentence is a limit a model averages away.

Three yeses

When a third person is involved, one rule carries the whole feature.

An invitation is three separate yeses, and any one no ends it.

Whoever raised it has said yes by raising it, and everybody else starts at not answered. One no closes it there and then, and a half who has said no is never asked again by the machinery. A new invitation clears the previous one's answers, because "we asked you in October" is not consent to anything in November.

Your yes requires your own message. A narrator writing you agreeing is rejected outright, because that is the narrator deciding what you want, which is the one thing it may never do. Even a fully agreed invitation is only an agreement, and the narrator is told so in as many words: it is agreed; it has not happened yet.

Afterwards, they are allowed to disagree

Each half can say separately what happened. The world does not adjudicate, because it does not know which of them is right and neither do they.

If the two accounts differ, both of them pay a small amount of warmth and trust, deliberately small, and it goes both ways. Nobody is lying. They simply had different evenings, and what that does to a couple is a slow thing rather than a row. The narrator gets both versions with one line attached: neither of them is lying.
