Rumour is arithmetic. Two people in the same room who know each other may pass on something one of them holds. Each hop lowers confidence, raises distortion and inflates numbers. No model is involved, and the same world at the same minute gossips identically every time.

How something travels

For a story to move from one person to another, three things must be true: they are in the same room, the teller knows the listener, and the teller holds the story. Strangers do not gossip.

Each hop costs the story something:

Numbers only ever grow in the retelling. When a name drifts, the substitute is drawn from people the listener knows, because a story mutates towards the world of whoever is holding it.

Once a version is mangled enough, the narrator is no longer given the figure at all, only that somebody has picked up a hint. A narrator handed a number will quote it.

At what pace

The rates were calibrated against a live save rather than guessed. The target: the table and maybe one more friend by the end of the night, the wider circle over a couple of days, and the whole street only for something genuinely explosive.

A thing seen and kept quiet is not news yet, so the half-life is counted from the first telling rather than from the event.

Who keeps a secret

Anything said to one person is said in confidence by default. Whether it holds is not a property of the secret. It is how the listener feels about whoever the secret is about.

SettingDefaultMeaning
Confidence holds at0.7Trust and warmth together at or above this: a flat no, for good
Leak per conversation0.08Below the threshold, scaled by how little they care

The hard line is deliberate. A small probability over a long enough game leaks everything, which would make asking pointless and make the person you trust most no different from a stranger. Once somebody has said it once, they have said it.

Two further rules. Nobody gossips their own secret. And nobody is told, at third hand and with the number tripled, about their own life.

The table-talk damper

People do not repeat a thing a friend told them across a table the way they repeat a thing they saw. If the person a story is about told you themselves, to your face, in a group of four or fewer, you are much less likely to pass it on, and the more you like them the less likely still. Somebody indifferent repeats it at about a third of the usual rate. Somebody fond of them, at a few per cent.

The damper covers only what they told you personally. What you saw is yours to tell, and so is hearsay. Announcing something to a whole room strips the protection entirely.

How a story goes quiet

Firsthand knowledge never decays. Secondhand belief halves every three days and below a floor is no longer held. So going quiet is a real move: the street's sense that there is money around you fades over a fortnight, while the people who saw it never stop knowing. The World screen reports both as a sentence: "five of eight: three saw it, two heard it, one has let it go".

You are not part of this

The player never receives gossip. The narrator may not decide what you feel, so the simulation does not decide what you believe either. Anything you know, somebody told you in a scene.

Where it surfaces

A world can make the rumour mill readable, for example as a neighbourhood feed on the in-game phone. Every post is a fact somebody in the city is holding, shown in the version the last person to pass it on was carrying, with names half filed off: an initial for somebody you know, a role for somebody you do not, and "the new guy" for you. A thing kept in confidence and never passed on does not appear.

Under the hood

Diffusion runs inside the tick, once per ten-minute step, and every roll comes from the seeded generator. A test asserts that the same day gossips identically twice. The rates above are one options object, and a world can override any of them.
