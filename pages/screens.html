The World screen is a window onto the simulation. It has almost no authority over it.

The clearest example is the button on a place that says "Go here". It does not move you. It types a sentence into the chat box, and you still press send. Your feet are yours on this screen as everywhere else.

Civitas has no front end of its own. It is driven by a chat app, which puts a World screen next to the conversation. This page describes that screen, because it is the only way to see that the machinery is really doing what the rest of the site claims.

The top bar

The world's name, the time and date on the world's own calendar, a dial showing the sun and moon on an arc, and the weather as an icon, a temperature and one clause.

Then the one genuinely game-like readout: the fact the world is tracking as it spreads, drawn as a row of pips with one shade for the people who saw it and another for the people who only heard, and a caption underneath. Nobody knows. Seven of twenty know.

Three views

Scene, the room you are in
The painting of that room at this hour, with a night wash and rain or fog over it outdoors. A line per person present and what they are doing this minute. A separate short list for people behind a door in the same flat, heard and not seen, so a line and not a face. Faces standing in the room, placed against the painting's own anchor points, so the woman working the taps is behind the bar and the cook is by the pass. Exits along the bottom with how many minutes away they are and whether they are shut.
District, the street as a cutaway
No coordinates are authored anywhere. Buildings are places whose parent is outdoors, rooms are their children, and a room's floor is read out of its name, so 4A is the fourth floor. Add a room to the world and it appears on the map with nobody touching the layout. Rooms go dark when empty at night, but never completely, because a window you cannot see into is a wall. People walking somewhere are drawn on the pavement outside the door they are heading for.
People, the web of feeling
Everybody as a face, with curved lines between them. Colour for the kind of feeling, thickness for trust, an arrowhead for who feels it. Each direction gets its own bowed line, because how she feels about him and how he feels about her are two different things. Hovering somebody dims everybody unrelated, because thirty people's feelings at once are a ball of wool.

Scene and District have a slider that runs twelve hours forward in quarter-hour steps and replays everybody's routine. It is careful about what it claims. It shows what people intend, not a forecast, because nobody's routine survives a reason to break it. You stay where you are on it, because where you will be is the one thing on that screen that is yours to decide.

The side panel

Tonight holds the evening's newest picture, who is due in or out, the agreements in force, and the last few things that happened. Situations lists what is unresolved, who is involved, what their stance is, and flags the ones the simulation found by itself rather than being authored.

Plans puts where you are right now at the top, because everything under it is read against that, then each arrangement with a live countdown, who it is with, and whose idea it was, because a date you did not ask for reads differently. Clashes are called out and hard to skim past. Broken plans are kept in a fold, since a plan you broke is the part you will want to find again.

Rumours has two halves: what you have picked up about other people and who told you, and what they say about you. The second is grouped by the fact rather than by the person, with a count, because one row per person made "two things are going round, not twenty-six" invisible. A story the town has stopped repeating is marked as being let go.

Journal is one fold per day, projected from the log, plus a gallery of every picture drawn in play, grouped by who is in it.

A person's sheet

Their portrait, at most two mood words, where they are and what is next. Four meters: warmth, trust, want, respect, drawn as steps rather than slopes over time, because feelings hold until they change.

Two details are worth pointing at. The trust bar carries small tick marks, titled "something unlocks here", so you can see that a threshold exists without being told what is behind it. And what somebody is keeping is shown as a row of locks with a line of guidance, never as the secret itself: trusts you enough for the next one, the moment has to be right.

That restraint is the rule everywhere on this screen. Counts and shapes, never the contents of what anybody keeps. A threshold is a number on a bar, not the secret behind it.

The game master's view

One switch, with a gate the first time you touch it: this shows what everybody knows, keeps and feels, and there is no unseeing it.

With it on, everybody is called by their true name, the web of feeling draws what people actually feel rather than what you could plausibly know, and a new section appears with a fold per character: everything that person holds, how they came by it, and how many other people hold the same thing, which is the whole shape of a rumour in one number.

It is read-only. It is there for somebody playing as their own author, and for anybody who wants to check whether the simulation is really doing what it says. The second use is the honest reason it exists.

On a phone

A narrow window gets a different layout rather than a squeezed desktop: a tab bar along the bottom, one thing on screen at a time, sheets instead of panels, and faces redrawn at thumb size and nudged apart so none of them overlap.

What the screen is behind on

Panels update a beat after the reply rather than in lockstep, and the world is sometimes one turn behind the prose by design. Waiting for it would cost the narrator its entire brief, so the world lands a turn late rather than never. Pictures arrive tens of seconds afterwards and are pinned under the message they belong to.
