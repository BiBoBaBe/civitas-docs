The World screen is a window onto the simulation. It has almost no authority over it. The one button that looks like it moves you, "Go here", types a sentence into the chat box and leaves you to press send.

The top bar

The world's name, the time and date on the world's own calendar, a dial showing the sun and moon, and the weather as an icon, a temperature and one clause. Then one readout that is genuinely game-like: the fact the world is tracking as it spreads, drawn as a row of pips in two shades, one for people who saw it and one for people who only heard, with a caption. "Nobody knows." "Seven of twenty know."

Three views

Scene: the room you are in
The painting of that room at this hour, with a night wash and weather over it outdoors. A line per person present and what they are doing. A separate short list for people behind a door in the same building, heard and not seen. Exits along the bottom with the minutes to each and whether it is shut.
District: the street as a cutaway
No coordinates are authored anywhere. Buildings are places whose parent is outdoors, rooms are their children, and a room's floor is read out of its name, so 4A is the fourth floor. Add a room to the world and it appears on the map. Rooms go dim when empty at night. People walking somewhere are drawn on the pavement outside the door they are heading for.
People: the web of feeling
Everybody as a face, with curved lines between them. Colour for the kind of feeling, thickness for trust, an arrowhead for who feels it. Each direction is its own bowed line, because how she feels about him and how he feels about her are two records. Hovering somebody dims everybody unrelated.

Scene and District have a slider that runs twelve hours forward in quarter-hour steps and replays everybody's routine. It shows what people intend, not a forecast, because nobody's routine survives a reason to break it. You stay where you are on it, because where you will be is the one thing on that screen that is yours.

The side panel

Tonight
The evening's newest picture, who is due in or out, the agreements in force, and the last few things that happened.
Situations
What is unresolved, who is involved, what their stance is, with the ones the simulation found by itself marked as found rather than authored.
Plans
Where you are now at the top, then each arrangement with a countdown, who it is with, and whose idea it was. Clashes are called out. Broken plans are kept in a fold.
Rumours
What you have picked up about other people and who told you, and what they say about you, grouped by the fact with a count. A story the town has stopped repeating is marked as let go.
Journal
One fold per day, projected from the log, plus a gallery of every picture drawn in play.

A person's sheet

Their portrait, at most two mood words, where they are and what is next. Four meters, drawn as steps rather than slopes over time, because feelings hold until they change. The trust bar carries tick marks titled "something unlocks here", so you can see that a threshold exists without being told what is behind it. What somebody is keeping is shown as a row of locks with a line of guidance, never as the secret itself.

That restraint is the rule everywhere on the screen: counts and shapes, never the contents of what anybody keeps.

The game master's view

One switch, with a warning the first time you touch it: this shows what everybody knows, keeps and feels, and there is no unseeing it. With it on, everybody is called by their true name, the web of feeling draws what people actually feel, and a fold per character lists everything they hold, how they came by it, and how many others hold the same thing.

It is read-only. It exists for somebody playing as their own author, and for anybody who wants to check whether the simulation is doing what this site says it does.

What the screen is behind on

Panels update a beat after the reply, and the world is sometimes one turn behind the prose by design: waiting for it would cost the narrator its whole brief, so the world lands a turn late rather than never. Pictures arrive tens of seconds afterwards and are pinned under the message they belong to.
