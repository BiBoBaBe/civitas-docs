A plan is the one thing that outranks somebody's day.

Somebody says come by tomorrow at eight. Tomorrow at half past seven she puts her coat on, leaves the bar she would otherwise be working, walks the eleven minutes, and is standing there at eight. If you are not, she waits, and then she thinks a little less of you.

Before arrangements existed, that sentence was written by the narrator, read by nobody, and quietly untrue by eight.

Why they beat a routine

An arrangement is the only thing in this engine somebody actually agreed to out loud, so it is the only thing that beats their timetable. They leave in time for it, give it an hour, and then their own day takes them back.

What an arrangement cannot beat is a scene you are standing in, because the story is always allowed to overrule the clock. The full order, top to bottom: a scene you are in, then a trip, then an arrangement, then an errand somebody took on, then their own day. An errand ranks under a meeting because a meeting is two people's evening and an errand is one.

How they behave

People set off half an hour early, plus the walk. That lead is also what keeps somebody on the other side of the city honest, since they are only reconsidered every few steps and would otherwise notice the hour after it had gone.

They give it an hour, unless you are in it and still in the room, in which case up to four, because leaving mid-scene is the bug the whole rule exists to prevent. Turning up counts within half an hour of the time. Keeping an appointment is a committing choice: somebody keeping one is not reweighing the bar against it every ten minutes.

One-off and standing

A one-off has an hour. A standing arrangement has a weekday pattern and no hour, because a standing arrangement is a rule about Thursdays and not a meeting on one. Each Thursday becomes a real meeting a day in advance, exactly once however many times the world looks at it.

You can say when in the way somebody would actually write it: tomorrow 20:00, tonight, in two hours, thursday 21:00, day 11 20:00. Weekdays resolve against the calendar the narrator has been reading all session rather than the engine's internal count, because a narrator that has seen "Day 8 (Thursday)" all evening will write "thursday" and mean that one. A phrase the world cannot read is refused rather than guessed at.

Kept and broken

Everybody there is kept. Somebody there and somebody not is broken, and the person who waited thinks a little less of the one who did not come: a small amount of trust and a little warmth. It is a disappointment, not a betrayal.

Nobody there at all is also broken, but nobody was left standing in the rain, so nobody feels anything about it.

The world never writes what you feel about being let down. That is yours. And a plan is settled wherever you happen to be, because a plan you never heard about again is exactly the one worth settling.

Trips

Everything else in this engine happens within walking distance. A plan is an evening, a routine is a day, and the router measures a city in minutes. Then you say you are flying somewhere on Friday, or ask somebody three hours away to come and stay for a week, and none of that machinery has anything to say.

A trip is not a new kind of object. It is a plan with a journey attached, so everything that already knows about plans knows about it. Four consequences follow, and they are the point.

A trip collides with your diary
Friday's date is a plan and a week away is a plan, and two plans that want you in two places is something the world can see and say out loud before you book it. It does not decide which one wins. It says so, loudly, and leaves it to you.
A trip is lived
The journey is a real route over the real map: forty-five minutes to the terminal, then twelve hours of aeroplane. The skip machinery lives it hour by hour and the city carries on without you while it does. The woman you stood up on Friday notices on Friday.
A trip costs
Money leaves the purse at the moment of booking, priced off the journey. A cancelled trip does not refund the fare, which is what an airline would also say, and which is what makes booking one a decision rather than a doodle. Paying for somebody else's flight is a transfer with their name on it, and that is how paying for people gets you found out in a world where not-being-known is the scarce thing.
A trip can be refused
She has a bar to run, a rota, a purse and an opinion of you.

Being told no

Whether somebody will get on an aeroplane for you is answered in the order a person answers it. Can she get there at all. Has she already got something on. Can she afford it, or are you paying. Does she want to. The refusal says which, in words somebody could actually text: she cannot get away; not for that long, not right now; she does not know him well enough to cross a border for him.

Somebody who has never met you is not getting on a plane for you, however warm the messages have been. Somebody who owns a business loses money by leaving it, and the world knows that from her job and her routine. A week does not cost seven times what a night costs, because leaving at all is most of the price.

What a visitor does to a small street

The most visible thing in a town where everybody knows everybody is somebody nobody knows. A woman going into your building with a bag is a story before she has taken her coat off, and it becomes an ordinary fact that spreads like any other and that you can be asked about.

Not until she actually arrives, though. A woman still in her own flat is nobody's news.

If you paid a large fare, she learns something too. Not the number, because she cannot see the number. She sees that a man she has never met bought a seat she could not have bought herself and did not ask what it cost, and she holds that as a guess. It travels like any other guess, and it can be wrong.
