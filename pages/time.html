The clock is fictional minutes since the world began, and it never looks at the real one.

Say you sleep until morning. The clock does not jump to seven. The world runs its ordinary tick in half-hour chunks all the way there, so the rota still turns over, two people keep an arrangement, somebody stands somebody else up, a rumour moves one more hop and an older one decays, and you get a text at one in the morning from somebody who could not sleep either.

Then you wake up and read what you missed, as a few lines out of your own journal.

The clock

Time moves because something said it should: a walk cost eleven minutes, a scene took three, the narrator said an hour passed, you asked to skip to Friday. It never advances because time passed for you.

A world can name its days so that the fiction and the clock agree. The example world opens on Day 8, a Tuesday, a week after the player signed for his flat. Every timestamp on screen is printed on that calendar, because a narrator whose header disagrees with the world's clock learns very quickly to ignore the clock.

Midnight is not an event. It is a number crossing a multiple of 1,440.

How a turn advances time

Three things can decide, in order. If the app states how long the turn took, that is it, including zero, where the clock does not move. Otherwise, if the narrator wrote that an hour passed, an hour passes. Otherwise the world uses a default beat, which is ten minutes generally and three in Tremont, because a bar conversation is not ten minutes long.

One extra rule. If you set off walking this turn, the turn ends when you arrive, so the next scene is the room you walked into and not a street you are halfway down.

Time the narrator says has passed is lived, not jumped. An hour tagged in a reply runs as an hour of world: people walk, shifts end, gossip moves. The alternative was tried, and because a tagged jump also replaced the beat, a narrator that tagged time every turn froze the whole street where it stood.

What a tick is

A tick is the world taking one breath. Standing arrangements turn into tonight's meetings, people decide where to be and start walking, bodies tire, whatever you are not watching resolves itself, gossip moves, the clock advances. Then the world asks what the hour has settled: which plans were kept, which were stood up, which agreements have lapsed, which tasks are due, which situations have quietly become true.

Long stretches are chopped into ten-minute steps with the remainder run as one short step. Without that remainder a three-minute exchange rounded down to nothing, and a world driven in short exchanges stood still at the minute it opened.

Travel does not move the clock. A journey is a departure time and an arrival time, so one person's walk across town cannot drag the city forward with them.

Skipping ahead

You can skip by a duration, until morning, until your next arrangement, or to a phrase like "friday 19:30" read against the world's own calendar. It stops early if something needs you:

  • An arrangement you are part of is about to start. It stops at the moment you would have to leave, not at the hour itself.
  • Somebody walks into the room you are in.
  • Somebody who is fond of you texts.

A skip can run at most a fortnight, and it can be asked where it would land without living any of it. What you missed comes back as up to a dozen journal lines, read off the log rather than summarised by a model, or honestly as "nothing that concerned him".

What gets simulated in full

Everyone keeps their timetable and everyone keeps their appointments, always. What varies is how much thought the world spends on them.

People near you get a full sweep every step: they weigh their options, chase what they want, change their minds. People far away are reconsidered every third step and otherwise keep to their day, which is most of what the sweep would have produced anyway.

Near means near in the web of relationships, not near in the street.

A creditor plotting against you from across the city is one hop away and deserves full simulation, while the stallholder in the next room is three hops away and does not.

Being in the room with you is itself a hop, so standing next to somebody still counts. So does owing them money, being in a situation with them, or having an arrangement with them that still stands. An arrangement kept last week is not a reason to go on simulating somebody in full.

None of this is written down. A stored tier is a cache nobody remembers to invalidate.

Weather

Weather is not stored either. It is worked out from the world's seed and the minute, so the same minute is always the same rain, a reroll cannot change it, and a thousand turns of it cost nothing. It drifts over five-hour blocks rather than flickering turn to turn: a handful of changes a day, not one a reply.

A world declares a climate, meaning a typical day and night temperature and how wet it is. Tremont is set to a cold wet autumn, which is the entire reason a dead boiler is a story.
