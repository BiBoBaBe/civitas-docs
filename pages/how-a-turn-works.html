Every turn follows the same five steps. The world writes a brief. The model writes the scene and tags what changed. The simulation checks every tag against the world. What survives is written to the log. Then the clock moves.

1. The world writes a brief

The brief is a query, not a dump. The world is never serialised and sent to the model. Instead the simulation asks, for this player at this minute in this room, what the model needs to know, and renders that as plain text inside a token budget.

Some lines are pinned: the time, the place, you, and everybody standing in the room with you. They survive any budget, because the model can never be allowed to forget somebody your character can see. Everything else competes for the space that is left, ranked by how much it bears on this moment. A debt due tomorrow outranks one due next month. A rumour about the person in front of you outranks one about somebody across town.

The sections, in the order they appear:

TIME, PLACE, YOU
The clock on the world's own calendar, the room and its one-line description, and whether the door is shut or locked. Then your name and what you are doing.
PRESENT
Everybody in the room, named the way you know them, with what they are doing and, where it matters, what they believe.
NEARBY
People in another room of the same building. The brief spells out the rule: they hear a raised voice and you hear them, but nobody sees through a door.
JUST ARRIVED, JUST LEFT, AWAY, VISITING
Movement since the last turn, so the model shows people coming and going instead of having them appear.
PLANS, TERMS, COUPLES, COLLAR
Arrangements that stand, agreements the world is holding people to, couples as a unit, and open tasks.
OPEN, RECENT, UNRESOLVED
Unfinished situations with their stakes and deadlines, the last few things that happened, and threads that are waiting on somebody.

The same world at the same minute always produces the same brief. That is what makes it possible to write a test for context assembly at all.

2. The model writes the scene

The model receives three things: a voice contract supplied by the world, which says how the prose should read; the brief; and your message. The voice contract carries nothing about what is true. Everything true arrives only through the brief, and a test asserts that the world appears nowhere else in the prompt.

Along with the prose, the model is required to emit short tags for anything that changed in the world. They sit on their own lines at the end of the reply and you never see them. The instruction is phrased as an obligation, because a scene the simulation never hears about is a scene that did not happen:

STATE CHANGES MUST BE TAGGED.
If anything in the world changed during your reply — somebody moved, time passed, a debt was
made or paid, a feeling shifted, a situation opened, advanced or ended, somebody learned
something — you MUST record it by putting the matching tag on its own line at the very end.
The player never sees these lines; they are how the world finds out what you did.

A tag looks like this. Double angle brackets, because square brackets are common in prose and in players' own asides:

<<move person="the clerk" place="The Anchor">>
<<pay person="Anna Reed" target="the clerk" amount="20">>
<<plan person="Anna Reed" place="The Anchor" when="tomorrow 20:00" what="settling up">>
<<door place="the back room" state="locked">>
<<advance_time minutes="20">>

There are thirty-three built-in tags, and a world can add its own. The full table is on What gets written down.

3. A second pass reads the prose

The model tags what it meant to do. A separate, cheaper pass, the scene reader, reads the finished prose for what the narrator did not bother to tag: what somebody is doing now, what came off, who has had a drink, whether a door was shut, whether somebody said one of the things they keep. It answers with the same tags, from a fixed list of names, so it cannot invent a person who is not in the room.

4. Every tag is checked

Nothing commits unchecked. Each tag is parsed, its names are resolved against the world, and its preconditions are tested against the world as it will be when that tag runs. Tags in one reply are applied in order to a scratch copy, so opening a situation and then advancing it works, while advancing one that was never opened does not.

A tag that fails is reported with a reason and a repair hint, and never reaches the log. The reasons are written for a writer rather than a programmer:

<<move person="Sam Vale" place="The Anchor">>
  — the narrator cannot move the player. let the player move himself

<<confide person="the cook" secret="why she left the city">>
  — they do not trust him enough to tell him that yet (0.12 of 0.45).
    let them keep it for now; it comes out when something between them has earned it

<<term person="Jade" mode="counter" ...>>
  — that is already signed. void it and draw up new terms instead of
    rewriting what somebody agreed to

When any tag is rejected, the engine asks the model once for a repair, keeping the prose and fixing only the listed tags:

Some of your tags did not work. Keep the prose, fix only these:
  <<move person="Sam Vale" place="The Anchor">> — the narrator cannot move the player. let the player move himself

After the repair pass the turn stands with whatever survived. Nothing is half-applied and nothing is silently dropped. Rejection is a normal case, not an error: every model gets a real share of tags refused, and the design expects it.

5. The clock moves and the world catches up

Time advances by what the turn cost. If Marinara says how long the turn took, that is used. If the model tagged that an hour passed, an hour passes. Otherwise the world uses a default beat, ten minutes by default and three in Tremont, because a bar conversation is short. The time is lived, not jumped: people walk, shifts end, gossip moves one step. Time and routines has the details.

Then the next turn starts with a fresh brief, rebuilt from the log.

Under the hood

The five steps map onto five modules: brief assembly, the narrator prompt, the verb parser, the adjudicator, and the tick. The adjudicator's contract is that a verb either applies cleanly or is refused with a reason, and a fuzz test throws thousands of random tags at it to check that refused verbs leave no trace and accepted ones leave the world satisfying every invariant. See The engine.

Rerolls and swipes

Deleting or swiping a reply does not edit the world. It rewinds it. The world forks at the moment before that reply was folded in, the clock goes back with it, and the abandoned version still exists on its own branch. A reroll is a different evening, not a corrected one.
