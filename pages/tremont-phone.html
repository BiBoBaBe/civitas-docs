The phone is not a mini-game. It is a different way of touching the same world.

Post a bounty on the tasks app and the money leaves your purse that second and sits in the task's own name. Somebody who is actually online, who can actually get there in time, takes it. At the deadline the world checks where they really were and what they really sent. If nobody took it, the money comes back. None of that is a separate system with its own bookkeeping.

Everything on this page was built for Tremont rather than being part of the engine.

A text is a real event

Messages are recorded word for word, never as a summary, so the thread has them and the narrator can be told afterwards what was traded rather than being made to invent it. Texts are witnessed by the two ends and by nobody standing in the room, because people do not read over your shoulder. But reading a message in company is a thing other people watch you do, so the room gets the moment and never the words.

The important decision is what happens when the person you are texting is standing in front of you.

In the room, and what you type is not a text exchange at all. It is you picking your phone up in front of her, and she is going to look up.

So a message to somebody present goes into your next turn as your own action and the scene plays it out. The phone tells you before you press, and the button changes to say so. A message to somebody elsewhere runs as its own quick conversation and costs the story no turn at all.

How long she takes to answer is a function of her evening rather than a random number, because that is what makes it readable. You learn that she is quick at eleven and gone by one, and learning that is the point of her having a life. Working a shift triples it. Company adds to it, and she is told that the people with her can see her looking at her phone but not what it says, so she should answer like somebody being watched.

Asleep is not a delay, it is a silence. The message stays owed, the thread says she has not seen it, and the moment the world wakes her the same message gets its answer.

Hush

An app where nobody is under their own name. You browse handles, message one, and eventually ask to meet.

Three things make it more than a menu. The distance shown is a real walking time over the real map, so two minutes away is a claim the world will honour when you walk it. Who is online is computed from the world: their character has to be on that app at all, the hour has to suit the day they are actually having, and they have to have the nerve for it, because somebody cautious is not on camera whatever their appetite for it. And it is drawn once per person per day, because being on an app is a thing you decide about an evening, not a coin flipped every ten minutes.

Asking to meet is a real decision by a real person, weighed on how she feels about you, how much you have actually talked, and how far she would have to come from wherever she genuinely will be at that hour. The refusals are specific. She will be asleep by then. She already has somewhere to be. She wants to talk more before she meets anyone. She is not feeling it tonight.

A yes is an arrangement the world keeps. She really goes.

Anonymity, and the two things it hides

There is only ever one person. The bartender you have been messaging has been the bartender the whole time, with all her feelings, her shifts, her plans and her side of every rumour attached to her. The handle is a mask on your screen, not a second set of books that would need keeping in step forever.

So two sentences are true at once, and neither knows about the other. The woman at the bar you have never been introduced to is "the bartender". The handle you have been messaging for a week is "@lastcall".

And there are two separate flags, because there are two separate facts. Whether she knows who you are is often true first: she saw you at the bar, you never saw her. It becomes true automatically the moment you do something under your own name, such as tipping by name or booking by name. Whether you know who she is only ever comes from working it out, and from your own message, because recognising her is a thing you do rather than a thing the narrator does to you. A wrong guess costs nothing and leaves no trace, because a guess said to your own phone is not a scene.

The narrator is instructed accordingly, and bluntly. When you do not know: never say so, and never use her name for the handle. When she knows and you do not: let it show in a look, a line only she would catch, or a private smile. Show, do not explain.

The tells are written down at the same moment the handle is, so the narrator has something to plant from the first message and something to pay off at the reveal. A tell invented at the reveal is a tell nobody was shown. One appears in her listing photograph each day. A lime under a thumbnail. A hospital lanyard in a picture she meant to crop. Flour under her nail at two in the morning. A ring in every photo, on purpose.

When you do work it out, it is not simply an unmasking. A fact is written that you both hold, marked as being between the two of you: we did that, and nobody else knows. Which means it is now an ordinary secret, and it can get out by the ordinary rules.

Collar

A tasks app, and the only asymmetric thing on the phone.

An arrangement is two people agreeing; a task is one person asking and the other answering, and the answer is the point.

It runs three ways. Somebody sets you one, and accepting or refusing needs your own message. Refusing is a real answer with a real price: she likes you slightly less for having asked, and thinks slightly more of you for hearing the answer. You set somebody one, and whether she does it is the world's call rather than yours. Or you post a bounty with nobody's name on it, and whoever is on the app, can get there in time, and is most willing takes it.

The world only believes what it can see. At the deadline it asks what it can actually observe: were they at the named place between being asked and the hour, and is there a message with a photo attached, sent to the setter, in the log. Words do not satisfy a request for a photograph. People leave it late, about half an hour before the hour, and a proof sent at the deadline and judged in the same moment counts, because that is not a race, it is the ordinary shape of leaving it late.

One design decision is worth pointing at. Fear pushes away, not towards. Willingness is built from warmth, trust and desire, minus fear, because a world where fear bought compliance would be a world where being frightening is a strategy.

The cam platform

Four things you can do, and each writes something real rather than playing a cutscene.

Watching has its own event with a witness list of exactly one person: the performer. She can learn you were there, be told about it, and bring it up. The room you are sitting in never finds out. Under your own name she forms an ordinary belief about you, which is a fact she can later tell other people, and that is exactly how "he watches her shows" gets out. Under a handle, no such belief forms.

Tipping moves real coin. What she learns is your choice. A named tip warms her a little because she knows who to be warm towards. An anonymous one warms her towards nobody she can find, which is the price of anonymity. The warmth is measured against a day's wage and kept small, because a tip is not a relationship.

Booking a private session is not a special object. It is an ordinary arrangement with a price, held in escrow, checked against whether she is actually performing at that hour and not booked more than two days out. Kept pays her. Her not turning up refunds you.

Asking to appear with her is deliberately not resolved by the machinery at all. It opens a situation, because what she makes of it, and whether it happens, and how, is a scene, and scenes belong to the narrator.

The neighbourhood feed

The rumour mill, made readable, and nothing more. Every post is a fact somebody in the city is actually holding, shown in the version the last person to pass it on was carrying. It drifts, its numbers grow, and it sometimes ends up pinned on the wrong person.

A fact only appears once it has travelled. A thing kept in confidence and never passed on is not on it, which is the whole point of confidence. Names are half filed off the way a neighbourhood app does it: an initial for somebody you know, a role for somebody you do not, and "the new guy" for you.

What is deliberately kept from the browser

Nothing a browser can read, including the app listings, the threads, the task lists, the feed and the journal, carries the name or the internal identifier behind a handle. The two halves of that join live in different places and neither holds the other's key, because one shared identifier would undo the whole anonymity layer in a single step.
