Principles

Civitas is ordinary engineering applied to a problem usually solved by asking a model to try harder. Seven rules run through the whole codebase. Each is listed here with what it means at the table and how it is enforced rather than hoped for.

1. State is structured, never prose

If something has to stay consistent, it is a record in the world, never a sentence in a summary. A debt is a number on a link between two people. A feeling is a number on an axis. A promise is a plan with a time on it.

At the table: a debt survives a week because it is a number, not because the model was careful.

Enforced by: the world has no free-text state. The only prose the engine holds is what it shows you back, such as a person's activity or a situation's one-line summary, and nothing is computed from it.

2. Most input never reaches a model

Walking somewhere, waiting, paying, checking the time, reading a phone thread: these are rules, resolved instantly and for free. The model is used for drama, not for lookups.

At the table: "Go to the bar" is a route over the map with a walking time, and you arrive without anybody having to write a paragraph about it.

Enforced by: the player's own actions have their own routes into the service, separate from narration, and the model is never asked to perform them.

3. The simulation is pure and seeded

The step that moves the world forward reads a world and returns the events that would happen, writing nothing. Every random choice draws from a generator seeded from the world, the branch and the sequence number. The same log always replays the same city.

At the table: a reroll cannot change the weather, and the same evening replays identically after a restart.

Enforced by: fictional time is a separate type, so the real clock cannot leak in without a compile error. Event identifiers are derived from position in the log rather than generated at random. Tests plan the same day twice and assert the results are identical.

4. The model proposes, the simulation adjudicates

Every change the model asks for is checked against the world before it is written. A change that fails is refused with a reason and never persisted.

At the table: a narrator that has somebody hand over a secret they have not earned gets a note back and a second try, and the secret stays kept.

Enforced by: every verb declares preconditions. The adjudicator runs them against a scratch copy of the world and returns accepted, rejected and failed lists. A fuzz test feeds it random tags and asserts that rejected verbs leave no trace.

5. Context is a query with a budget, not a dump

The world is never sent to the model verbatim. Each turn is a query for what this scene needs, ranked by relevance, cut to a token budget, with the things that cannot be dropped pinned first.

At the table: a world with forty people and a hundred facts still costs a few hundred tokens a turn, and the person in front of you is never left out to make room.

Enforced by: the brief assembler takes a budget and a floor. A test passes it a budget of one token and checks the scene still names the room and everybody in it.

6. Everything is branchable

Swipes are branches. Branches are worlds. Rewinding to before a reply is a fork, not an edit, and the abandoned branch keeps existing.

At the table: swipe a reply away and the clock goes back, the people go back, and the rumour that started in the abandoned version never started.

Enforced by: the store is append-only. A fork writes one row and shares everything before it with its parent. Branch names are derived from where they forked, so replaying a session produces the same branches.

7. Level of detail everywhere

How much thought the world spends on somebody scales with how close they are to you, measured in the web of relationships rather than in metres. Somebody who owes you money across town is one hop away. A stranger in the next room is three.

At the table: forty people keep their timetables and their appointments every tick, but only the handful who matter to you right now reconsider their plans every step.

Enforced by: the tier is computed fresh each tick from the graph and never stored, because a stored tier is a cache nobody remembers to invalidate.

Two rules that follow from the seven

The player is inviolable. The narrator may not move you, spend your money, undress you, decide what you feel, decide what you believe, or claim you agreed to something. The simulation will not walk you on your timetable and will not pass you gossip. You learn things by being told, in the fiction, like a person. This is a property of the player's record rather than a check scattered across call sites, because it was once three separate checks and all three leaked.

No route returns the world. The bridge to Marinara can ask for a brief, a view, or a phone screen. It cannot fetch the graph. A bridge that can reach into the world is a bridge that eventually will, and then two programs own the truth instead of one.

Where these come from

The seven rules are the second section of the engine's design document, and each of the enforcement notes above points at a real module or test. The engine walks through the modules in order.
