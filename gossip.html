Gossip

Nobody is a database. A person can only repeat what they hold, and what they hold is what they will say out loud.

Forty coins

A clerk is owed forty and watched it not get paid, so he holds it exactly: forty, full confidence, no distortion. He tells the cook, who now says something like forty-five. She tells the welder, who says fifty-nine. He tells a broker, who says eighty.

Four people, one debt, no liars, and the original fact still says forty. By the third or fourth hop the story has drifted far enough that the narrator is not given the figure at all, only that somebody has picked up a hint about it, because a narrator handed a number will quote it.

How something travels

Two people have to be in the same room, and one has to actually know the other. Strangers do not gossip. No model is involved anywhere in this. It is arithmetic, and the same world at the same minute produces the same evening every time.

Each hop costs the story something. Confidence drops by about a fifth. Distortion rises. And numbers grow.

Numbers only ever grow. Nobody ever shortens a debt in the telling.

Names drift too. A quarter of the time, scaled by how mangled the story has got, the wrong person's name gets attached, and the substitute is drawn from people the listener knows, because a story mutates towards the world of whoever is holding it.

At what pace

This was calibrated against a real save rather than guessed. The target was the table and maybe one more friend by the end of the night, the wider circle over a couple of days, and the whole street only for something genuinely explosive.

A person tells one story to one person a day. People do not work a room with a piece of news; they tell a friend, and maybe another tomorrow. Two things add one telling each: a story that is genuinely scandalous, and a person whose character says they are a gossip. Three a day is the ceiling.

Nobody repeats what they have just heard, and how long they sit on it depends on how ordinary it is. Something explosive goes round the same hour. A middling story waits about four hours. A dull one waits until tomorrow.

News gets old. A story is told less the longer it has been going round, on a half-life of about a day and a half, counted from the first time anybody passed it on rather than from when the thing happened. A thing seen and kept quiet is not news yet. The clock starts when it starts moving.

And how interesting a thing is counts twice over rather than once. Under a straight line, a bottle bought at the bar travelled as far in three days as a scandal did.

The table-talk damper

There is a difference between a thing you saw and a thing somebody told you across a table.

People do not repeat a thing a friend told them across a table the way they repeat a thing they saw.

If the person a story is about told you themselves, to your face, in a small group, you are much less likely to pass it on, and the more you like them the less likely still. Somebody indifferent repeats it at about a third of the usual rate. Somebody fond of them, at a few per cent.

The damper covers only what they told you personally. What you saw is yours to tell. So is what a friend told you about them, because hearsay is fair game. Announcing something to a whole room strips the protection entirely, which is the right answer: a thing you said to eleven people is not a confidence.

Who keeps a secret

Asking somebody to keep something to themselves is not a switch that gets flipped.

Whether it holds is not a property of the secret: it is how this person feels about whoever it is about. Ask somebody who likes you and it holds. Ask somebody who does not and you have told the street slowly.

Above a threshold of regard, meaning trust and warmth taken together, it is not a low probability. It is a flat no, for good. Below it, a kept secret has a half-life: about an eight per cent chance per conversation, scaled by how little they care, and once they have said it once they have said it.

The hard line is deliberate. A small probability and a long enough game leaks everything eventually, which makes asking pointless and makes the person you trust most no different from a stranger.

Two more rules keep it honest. Nobody gossips their own secret, because a man hiding the bar's books from his wife does not bring it up with the regulars. And nobody is told, at third hand and with the number tripled, about their own life.

How a story goes quiet

This is the part most systems have no answer for. Firsthand knowledge never decays: the people who were standing there never stop having seen it. Secondhand belief halves every three days and below a floor is no longer held at all, because it has stopped being worth repeating.

Going quiet is therefore a real move. The street's sense that there is money around you fades over a fortnight. The four people who actually saw it never stop knowing. The world can tell you both numbers as a sentence rather than a count: five of eight, three saw it, two heard it, one has let it go. Or simply, nobody repeats it any more.

You are not part of this

The player never receives gossip. Not once, from anybody, ever. The narrator may not decide what you feel, so the simulation must not decide what you believe either. You learn things by being told, in the fiction, like a person.

Where it surfaces

Rumour is not only a hidden number. Tremont puts a neighbourhood feed on the in-game phone, which is the rumour mill made readable and nothing more. Every post is a fact somebody in the city is actually holding, shown in the version the last person to pass it on was carrying. It drifts, its numbers grow, and it is sometimes pinned on the wrong person.

A thing kept in confidence and never passed on does not appear on it, which is the whole point of confidence. Names are half filed off the way a neighbourhood app does it: an initial for somebody you know, a role for somebody you do not, and "the new guy" for you.
