Tremont

Money is not the scarce resource. Not-being-known is.

The premise

The player character is worth a hundred million and nobody on his street knows it. He signed for flat 3B at 214 Holloway a week ago. The building has four floors of brick, a buzzer panel with three names taped over, and a dead boiler. His bathroom has new fittings and no hot water. It is a wet autumn.

He also has a penthouse across the river, which is one button in a lift and too much quiet. Nobody on Holloway knows that either.

The world opens on a Tuesday evening at the bar on the corner:

The Seven has the door propped open and the bar is three deep in regulars who all know each other. Nobody here knows a thing about you, which is the only part of this you own.

How a secret behaves when it is not a stat

His worth is a fact in the world, true from the first minute, and it is worth nothing at all until somebody holds a belief about it. Exposure is not a number anybody maintains. It is a count of who believes it, measured against the people whose knowing would count as the street knowing.

At the start, exactly one person in the world holds it: his driver, and he holds it in confidence.

Three things give it away, and none of them is telling somebody.

A place. Anybody who has ever stood in the penthouse knows. Not while they are in it, but ever, because you do not unsee a penthouse by walking out of it. The tower's lobby is deliberately not on that list, since a courier at the front desk learns nothing.

An amount. A single payment over five thousand, seen by anybody in the room. Rent on Holloway is four figures. Five in one movement, in front of people, is an answer to a question nobody asked.

Company. Standing on Holloway next to a man who drives him and calls him sir, on a street where nobody has a driver. That one is vaguer. They see the money, not the number, so they hold it as a guess, and a guess travels and can be wrong.

There is a fourth, which is you. The world keeps a short list of the kinds of thing you might type that give it away in part: I sold my company, money is not a problem, I could just pay for the whole building.

Telling somebody deliberately is a separate act, and it can be asked to stay quiet, which holds or does not depending on how they feel about you. See Gossip.

The four situations, and why the cheap route is the expensive one

Holloway opens with four unfinished things. Each has two routes: write a cheque, or work it through other people. Every cheque spends the only thing he cannot get back.

The situationWhat would count as it being over
The boiler. The quote is more than the building earns in a season, and the landlady has not said no to a developer.She has the money, or somebody has put a boiler in the basement.
The bar's quarter. The owners are one bad quarter from losing it. She knows. He knows more than he has told her.There is enough behind the bar to see the quarter out, in either of their pockets. It is the bar that is short, not a particular pocket.
The rota. A nurse has worked nights for two years. The rota is set by a scheduler at the hospital and she has stopped asking.Her own day stops putting her at the hospital at nine in the evening. By a word, a bribe, or quitting. The condition does not care which.
The studio. A trainer shoots in the corner of her bedroom with the bed pushed out of frame.She works somewhere that is neither her bedroom nor the gym she already coaches at.

Nothing anywhere enumerates the solutions. Rent her the basement, hand over the bar's back room, have a word with the scheduler, or do something nobody thought of, and the same check notices. Enumerating routes is what makes a world feel like a menu.

One of those conditions has a lesson attached. The first version of the studio one said only "not her bedroom", and it was satisfied before the world had finished loading, because a trainer works at a gym. A condition that is true at the start is a situation that never existed.

The cast, which is really a wiring diagram

Every person in the four districts is keeping exactly one thing, and the things are wired to each other. Here are three, all of them about money or work.

The bartender who has not been paid

She has not cashed her last three pay cheques from the bar, because she closes the till every night and knows they would bounce. Which means she knew the bar was in trouble before its owner did. It is also why the books look slightly better than they are, since three weeks of her wages are still in them. Her stance on the bar's situation is recorded, literally, as covering.

The offer on the building, which is four people long

The landlady has had a developer's offer for 214 and has not said no. A realtor forty minutes out carried that offer to her, and holds it in confidence because it is a client's business, which holds exactly as long as she goes on thinking well of the landlady. That realtor's commission on it is what would pay for her husband's church roof, which is fifteen thousand short, and he has been letting his congregation believe the fund is their giving rather than her envelopes. The developer behind it is backed by a financier in the same cul-de-sac whose promotion depends on the block being assembled before the year is out. And the law firm acting for that developer employs a litigator who is not yet a partner, whose buy-in the firm would waive for whoever lands a big enough client.

So the fastest way to keep a building standing is to fill a church roof fund forty minutes away, and nobody on Holloway would ever learn why the pressure eased. That is not a scripted branch. It is four separate people's conditions happening to have the same answer.

A loan between cousins, blocking an introduction

A chef lent her cousin six thousand to keep a gym's doors open and has not asked for it back. Across town, a streamer needs one collaboration with somebody bigger, and the only social chain to that person runs through the chef and the cousin. The chef will not ask for a favour while the loan sits between them. Meanwhile the person she wants to reach has her own next want, which is satisfied by meeting anybody new at all. Two people want the same thing from opposite ends and neither can see the other.

Not one of those has anything to do with romance. The machinery underneath is about obligations, information and who is standing where, and the cast is written so that pulling any thread the hard way pulls two others with it.

The four districts

Holloway is the street he lives on. One walk-up, a corner bar, a gym, and a hospital fifteen minutes away. Nine residents. Its problems are four figures.

Kessler is across the bridge, eighteen minutes. Glass and stone, a courthouse, a members' club behind an unmarked door, a law firm, and the tower his penthouse is in. Its problems are six figures, and a cheque here is not a kindness somebody notices, it is a transaction somebody's lawyer notices.

Wyneshire is the university quarter, twelve minutes up the line. A campus, a coffee shop, a tattoo parlour, the cheapest bar in the city and a creaky share house. Almost everybody here is also keeping up an act, so a cheque solves the situation and leaves the act standing.

Maplewood is forty minutes out by car. A cul-de-sac, a residents' association, a church with a bad roof and a country club with a wedding booked. Holloway is short of money and says so. Maplewood is short of money and would rather die.

Geography as a security measure

One piece of map design is worth pointing at, because it solves a problem with topology instead of a special case.

Standing in the tower gives the secret away, and the tower is on Kessler Row. So the Row is two minutes from the tower and eighteen minutes from Holloway, while Holloway to the tower is twenty. The fastest route between the two districts therefore never passes through the tower lobby. The doorman stands under the canopy on the Row rather than inside. Nobody is ever scheduled in there. A test checks both halves: that exactly one person still holds the secret after four days, and that no route between the districts goes through the lobby.

Gossip crosses only through a named person

News moves between people who share a room, so it can only cross a district if a body does. The world supplies those bodies deliberately.

Two Kessler professionals drink at the Holloway bar because nobody there knows their name, carefully on different nights, because each would recognise the other in a second. A couple commute in and out through Holloway every working day and have a drink while they wait for each other, and her timetable entry for that hour reads "telling him everything she heard today". A courier is in more rooms in a day than anybody in either district; her round drops an envelope at the tower's front desk and stops at the kerb, which is also pinned by a test.

And elsewhere

Ten more people exist who cannot walk to him. A bar owner in Palma, a night-shift radiologist two hours away, a gym owner in Berlin, cabin crew whose honest answer to "where are you" is a hotel in a city she cannot remember the name of. He reaches them through his phone, and they carry on whether or not he opens it. A woman asleep in Palma at four in the afternoon is asleep in Palma at four in the afternoon.

Two of the ten are not far away at all, which is deliberate. One has had a bakery four doors from his own building for eleven years, and their sleeping hours mean they have never once been awake at the same time. The app did not find him a stranger. It found him a neighbour.
