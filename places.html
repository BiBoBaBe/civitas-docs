Places, doors and walls

The most useful thing this engine models is a shut door.

The night the gym had no rooms

Two people were in the private studio at the back of the gym with the door locked, and the prose said so over and over. But the gym was one place in the world, with no rooms in it, so everybody in the building was in the room with them. A woman working out on the floor witnessed all of it. She came away believing things that had never happened in front of her, and she could tell the street.

Rooms exist because of that evening. Everything below is the machinery that came out of it.

The shape of a place

Places nest. A room is inside a flat, the flat is inside a building, the building is on a street, the street is in a district. Streets connect with a number of minutes on them, and getting somewhere is the fastest route by time rather than the fewest turnings.

A place can have opening hours and a capacity. A shut place refuses you as a destination and also as a shortcut through. The router is the only thing standing between a timetable and somebody walking into a locked building at four in the morning. But closing time only stops people coming in. Nobody is thrown out at closing, because otherwise the bar is a cell.

While somebody is walking they are genuinely nowhere. The old room ended when they left, the new one begins when they arrive, and "on the road, arriving at 18:20" is a real answer rather than a gap to paper over. Somebody in transit sees nothing at all.

Who can see

A room is the unit of sight. You witnessed something if you were standing in the same room at the minute it happened. Nothing else counts, and nothing is stored: it is a question asked of the history each time, so it can never fall out of step with the history.

Who can only hear

Being in the same building but a different room is its own state. Within earshot, out of sight. The narrator gets those people in a separate block with the rule spelled out: they hear a raised voice and he hears them, but nobody sees through a door.

What you get depends on the door. Open, and you know which room they are in and what they are doing. Shut or locked, and you get only that somebody is in there. A door works both ways, so your own being shut hides them from you exactly as theirs hides you from them.

Whether a building is one earshot or several has to be declared, because nothing about the map can work it out.

A flat is its own premises. What happens in 4A is nothing to do with 1A, however thin the floor. A gym is not four premises because it has four rooms: the private studio, the locker room, the office and the floor are one gym, and the woman racking plates outside the door knows perfectly well who went in, even with the door shut.

A door is open unless somebody shut it in the prose, and open again the moment the room is empty, because a shut door in a room nobody is in is just a door. Locking one is the only thing in the engine that stops a timetable walking somebody in on a scene.

Who merely notices

Then the third case, which is where this page started. Two people go into a back room together and close it. Everybody left outside learns exactly one sentence: who went in, where, and whether the door shut. It is a bigger deal in a public building than in somebody's own flat, where the flatmate knows and the street does not care.

So the woman on the gym floor is left holding something true, interesting and incomplete, which she is free to repeat. What happened in there is not hers. That two people wanted a locked door is.

Volume: in the same room, and still not heard

Standing in a room is not the same as hearing what was said in it. Anything said out loud has an audience, and the default audience is the person being spoken to.

The world had no such idea: anything anybody said out loud was heard, in full, by everybody standing in the room, which is true of a toast and of a row at the bar and of nothing else people do.

There are two settings and no third. A thing is between you two, or it is not. Said to the room, everybody present holds it. Said to one person, only that person holds it, plus anybody the prose actually shows catching the words.

Everybody else in the room gets a roll, and what they can win is not a fuzzy version of the secret. It is a different, true statement: she told him something she does not tell people. It carries no subject, so nothing about her leaks through it. The words leak only when the prose says somebody heard them.

The chance of noticing at all is generous, around seven in ten, because being seen leaning in is the ordinary case. It falls with every extra head in the room. A packed bar is cover. A gym floor with one other person on it is not.

One more thing falls out of this. Anything said to one person is said in confidence by default, which switches on the machinery that decides whether the listener passes it on. That is Gossip.

How far you can take this: a moving room

None of what follows is required. A world runs perfectly well with streets and rooms and nothing else. This is what it looks like when you decide one particular detail is worth the trouble, and it is worth reading even if you never want a vehicle, because every part of it generalises.

A place can be marked as one you ride inside. That marker is the whole of the feature. The place itself is an ordinary room with its own door, so everything already on this page applies to it unchanged, and the engine has no special case for vehicles anywhere.

A moving room is still a room. While you are in it you genuinely have a location, and the location is the vehicle. Getting out is arriving. What is said inside is heard by the people inside and by nobody on the pavement, for exactly the reason a flat works that way.

A place can have a person who comes with it. Mark who drives, and that person is present, which means they hear everything said in the back. That is the point of the feature rather than a leak: what they hear is something they then know, and it travels like anything else they know. The same field gives you a carriage with a coachman, a boat with a boatman, a lift with an operator, a palanquin with bearers, a ship with a pilot. Anything that arrives with somebody attached.

It does not have to be useful. A vehicle with nobody driving it still works as a place. It simply never goes anywhere, and the engine refuses to travel in it. That is how you model a possession you can be shown, sit in and talk in. The general point is broader: a place does not have to take you anywhere to be worth having.

A place can be marked conspicuous. This is the one to steal. It is the only way the world can tell two things that do an identical job apart when one draws eyes and the other does not, and it has nothing to do with vehicles. A coat. A horse. A bodyguard walking two steps behind. A signet ring. The question "does this get me looked at" is separate from the question "does this get me there", and most worlds need somewhere to put the first one.

Then the part that makes it a mechanic rather than a detail. If somebody has more than one, the one they take is a decision, and the decision is a tell. The loud one. The one nobody looks at twice. The one they drive themselves, so that there is no witness at all.

Anybody who learns to read that choice knows something before a word is said. That only means anything in a world that was already counting who else was in the room, which is the argument for all of this: the depth is cheap because the machinery underneath it was already there.

Arriving somewhere

When your own message says where you are going, the narrator is told about it before you get there: how far it is, whether it will still be open, who will be there by their own timetable, and who will be audible in the next room.

Without that, a narrator arriving somewhere it knew nothing about invents the staff, and an invented receptionist is somebody the world has never heard of. Name a place that does not exist and the world says so rather than letting one be invented. Name two that match and it refuses to pick for you, and lets the scene make you say which.
