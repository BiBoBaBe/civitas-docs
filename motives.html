Wants and motives

A timetable tells you where somebody will be. A want tells you why they are somewhere else.

A man in a bar who should be at the gate

A clerk is owed forty coins. His timetable has him at the gate until six. At eight he walks into the bar where the woman who owes him is drinking, and sits down.

Nothing scripted that. His pressure to settle unfinished business rises as the deadline approaches, until it outweighs his own working day, and the bar is where she is. The world can say why in a sentence: he wants money because forty coins are outstanding.

Wants are worked out, not stored

Four pressures, computed fresh from the world every time they are asked for. Rest, money, duty and company.

The obvious design is a number on each person that decays every tick. That means a stream of events saying nothing, and a second copy of the truth to keep in step with the first. Computing them instead costs a few queries, cannot drift, replays identically, and comes with its own explanation attached.

That explanation is the part that earns it. Every reading carries the sentence that justifies it, in words a person could say. He has nowhere to sleep. Forty coins are outstanding. Which is what makes something that happened off-screen feel caused rather than rolled, because the world can always be asked why.

What each one measures:

  • Rest is how long since they were last at their own bed. Somebody with nowhere to sleep reads zero, because it is not a pressure they can act on.
  • Money is what they owe.
  • Duty is the worst unfinished thing they are pushing on, scaled by how near its deadline is. This is the one that makes a creditor turn up.
  • Company is zero if somebody they know is in the room, and otherwise not.

How a want becomes something they do

Every so often, a person who is near enough to you to be worth simulating in full weighs their options. Keeping to the day is worth a fixed amount. Standing idle is worth less. Going to settle unfinished business is worth whatever their duty pressure is, and it targets the place where the other party is actually standing.

The rule that holds it together is one sentence: the routine is the floor, not the law.

The weights are set so that ordinary life usually wins, which is the whole point:

  • Exhaustion only overrides a working day once it is genuinely bad. People go to work tired.
  • Wanting a thing is worth less than the day, deliberately. Wanting something is not a reason to walk out of a shift. It is a reason to drift towards it when the day is slack.
  • Chasing a thing goes to where the thing is, not where its owner is, because a purse left on a bench is easier than a purse in a pocket.
  • Somebody mid-task does not weigh anything at all. A person who wanders off halfway through has not made a decision; they have been walked away by a scheduler that did not know better.
  • A small amount of jitter keeps identical people from moving in lockstep.

Above all of it sit the things somebody actually agreed to. An arrangement outranks a want. A scene you are standing in outranks everything.

A want somebody else can act on

Wants are internal. A task is the version you can hand to another person, and it is the first thing in the engine that is not symmetric.

An arrangement is two people agreeing; a task is one person asking and the other answering, and the answer is the point.

Whether somebody does what you ask is the world's call rather than yours. She weighs how she feels about you, any willingness her character declares, and what it pays, against the size of the ask: the evidence demanded, the walk, and how little time she has. A place she cannot reach in time makes it impossible rather than merely hard.

One rule in there is worth stating on its own. Fear pushes away, not towards. Warmth, trust and desire raise willingness and fear lowers it, because a world where fear bought compliance would be a world where being frightening is a strategy.

When a want is blocked, it becomes a situation

This is the step that turns motive into story.

A condition is an ordinary fact: she owes him forty. It can sit there for years meaning nothing. A situation is a condition somebody has decided to do something about. It has stakes, it has sides with a stance each, and it pulls people across the map towards one another. The debt did not change on the day the situation opened. He did.

Situations open three ways. The narrator opens one in a scene. One ends badly and leaves a grudge behind, which is a rule rather than something anybody has to remember. Or the world finds one already sitting in the graph.

That last one is worth explaining, because nothing invents it:

Authored situations run out and ladders are finite. What does not run out is the graph: with a dozen people, asymmetric feelings, shared rooms and gossip that distorts, certain configurations simply are stories, and they keep arriving because every turn moves edges around.

The world scans for three shapes. Two people who know each other and are both drawn to the same third, where at least one of them wants rather than merely likes. Somebody carrying a badly mangled version of a fact about somebody else, firmly enough to repeat it. And somebody who has learned something about you that you did not tell them.

It is a query, not a guess. A situation exists because two specific links exist, so it can always be traced back to them, and it arrives at the moment it became true. At most a handful are open at once, so the brief stays a brief.

And what somebody wants next

Solve somebody's problem and the next thing they are after becomes live, usually the thing the first one was in the way of. A trainer with nowhere to work wants a room; give her one and she wants an audience; two rungs later she wants to not need you.

The next rung only opens if the one below it resolved. A situation that collapsed did not deliver the thing the next rung stands on, and somebody whose bar closed is not now worrying about its licence.

Optional depth

A world with people, places and timetables already works, and people will already be somewhere for a reason. Wants are what you add when you want the world to push back: somebody turning up where you did not ask them to, because something they needed outgrew their own afternoon.
