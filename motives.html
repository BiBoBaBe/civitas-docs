Wants and situations

A timetable says where somebody will be. A want says why they are somewhere else. The world computes four pressures for every person, fresh each time it asks, and each comes with the sentence that explains it.

Four pressures, computed not stored

type DriveId = "rest" | "money" | "duty" | "company";

interface DriveReading {
  id: DriveId;
  value: number;     // 0..1 pressure
  because: string;   // why it is that high, in words a person could say
}
Rest
Hours since they were last at their own bed, over 24. Somebody with nowhere to sleep reads zero, because it is not a pressure they can act on.
Money
What they owe, over 100.
Duty
The worst unfinished thing they are pushing on, scaled by how near its deadline is. This is the one that makes a creditor turn up.
Company
Zero if somebody they know is in the room, otherwise 0.5.

The obvious design is a number on each person that decays every tick. That produces a stream of events saying nothing and a second copy of the truth to keep in step with the first. Computing the pressures instead costs a few queries, cannot drift, replays identically, and comes with its own explanation. "He wants money because forty coins are outstanding" is a sentence the world can produce, and that is what makes something that happened off-screen feel caused rather than rolled.

How a want becomes something they do

Every so often, each person near enough to you to be simulated in full weighs a short list of options and takes the best one. Keeping to the day is worth a fixed amount, and everything else has to beat it.

OptionWorth
Keep to the timetable0.45
Stand idle when the timetable has no opinion0.15
Go home to restrest × 0.6, and only once rest is above 0.8
Settle unfinished businessthe duty pressure, aimed at where the other party actually is
Chase something wantedwant × 0.5, aimed at where the thing is
Find companythe company pressure
Jitter±0.05, so identical people do not move in lockstep

The weights are set so that ordinary life usually wins. People go to work tired. Wanting a thing is not a reason to walk out of a shift; it is a reason to drift towards it when the day is slack. Somebody in the middle of doing something does not weigh anything at all, because a person who wanders off mid-task has not made a decision. And above the whole table sit the things somebody actually agreed to: an arrangement outranks a want, and a scene you are standing in outranks everything. See Plans, tasks and trips.

The smallest example

A clerk is owed forty. His timetable has him at the gate until six. As the deadline approaches, his duty pressure rises until it outweighs 0.45, and at eight he walks into the bar where the debtor is drinking. Nothing scripted it. The world can say why in a sentence.

Situations

Three things are easy to confuse, and the engine keeps them apart.

  • A condition is a plain fact: she owes him forty. It can sit there for years meaning nothing.
  • A belief is what one person holds about that fact, and it does not update when the fact does. See What people know.
  • A situation is a condition somebody has decided to do something about. It has stakes from 0 to 1, a deadline, and sides with a stance each. It feeds the duty pressure and pulls people towards each other.

The debt did not change on the day the situation opened. The creditor did.

Situations open three ways. The narrator opens one in a scene with a tag. One ends badly and leaves a grudge behind, which is a rule rather than something anybody has to remember. Or the world finds one already sitting in the graph.

Situations the world finds by itself

Authored situations run out. What does not run out is the graph. With a dozen people, one-way feelings, shared rooms and gossip that distorts, certain configurations simply are stories, and they keep arriving because every turn moves the links around. The world scans for three shapes:

  1. Two people who know each other and are both drawn to the same third person, where at least one of them wants rather than merely likes.
  2. Somebody carrying a badly mangled version of a fact about somebody else, firmly enough to repeat it.
  3. Somebody who has learned something about you that you did not tell them.

It is a query, not a guess. A situation found this way exists because two specific links exist, so it can always be traced back to them, and it arrives at the moment it became true. At most a handful are open at once, so the brief stays a brief. The World screen marks these as found rather than authored.

How a situation ends

The narrator can close one with a tag. A situation can also end by itself when it has quietly become true, and what counts as the ending is declared as a condition rather than as a list of solutions. A trainer who shoots in her bedroom needs somewhere to work that is neither her bedroom nor her gym. Rent her a basement, lend her a back room, or do something nobody thought of, and the same check notices. Enumerating routes is what makes a world feel like a menu.

What somebody wants next

A world can give a person a ladder: when one situation of theirs resolves, the next rung becomes live, usually the thing the first one was in the way of. Give somebody a room and they want an audience. Two rungs later they want to not need you. Nothing opens until the rung below it closed, and only if it resolved rather than collapsed. Somebody whose bar closed is not now worrying about its licence.

Tasks: a want somebody else can act on

A task is one person asking and the other answering. Whether somebody does what you ask is the world's call, not yours. They weigh how they feel about you, any willingness their character declares, and what it pays, against the size of the ask: the evidence demanded, the walk, and how little time they have. Somewhere they cannot reach in time makes it impossible rather than hard.

One rule in that calculation is worth stating on its own. Willingness is built from warmth, trust and desire, minus fear. Fear pushes away, not towards, because a world where fear bought compliance would be a world where being frightening is a strategy.

Optional depth

A world with people, places and timetables already works, and people will already be somewhere for a reason. Wants, ladders and the situation scanner are what you add when you want the world to push back.
