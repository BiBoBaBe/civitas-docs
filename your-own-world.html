Making your own world

A world is a list of places, a list of people, and a few things that are unfinished.

The engine has no opinions about setting. It knows that people are somewhere, that they go somewhere else, that they believe things, that they want things, and that some of those things are unresolved. Whether the unresolved thing is a dead boiler, a blackmail letter, a blood feud or a floor boss makes no difference to any of the machinery.

The smallest world that ships with the engine is eight people, six rooms and one debt, and there is enough in it to watch for an hour.

Two ways to write one

There is a world card, which is a file of plain data anybody can write and trade. And there is a world written in code, which is what Tremont is. They are not equivalent, and the difference is worth knowing before you start.

What a world card contains

A name and an opening. The opening is the first thing a player reads before anybody has spoken. Without one, the first turn is spent working out where you are, which wastes the one moment the world has somebody's full attention.

Places. Each with a description, whether it is public, its opening hours, which other places it connects to, how many minutes away those are, and a capacity. Connections are two-way, so you write a street once.

People. A role, where they live, where they work, or an explicit day hour by hour. Who they know and how well. What they hold. What they want. What they belong to. And how they feel about specific other people, on each of the five axes.

Situations. What is unfinished: a kind, who is on each side, a one-line summary, the stakes, a deadline, an amount.

What has been misheard. One line that gives a named person the wrong version of a situation. This is the most characteristic thing a card can carry, and it would otherwise be several objects assembled by hand. One line, and the debt she is owed forty for is two hundred as far as the welder is concerned.

Resources, factions and items. What the world counts, what bodies people belong to, and objects that can be owned and handed over.

Leave out somebody's day and the importer works one out: sleep, work, an evening at the first public room. It then tells you in plain English every single thing it worked out for you. That is deliberate. A schedule you did not write is a convenience right up until somebody is in the wrong place and you cannot tell whether that was you or the tool.

It refuses rather than half-loading

A card is checked before anything is built, and you get every problem at once rather than the first one. If anything is fatal it will not load.

A card that half-loads is worse than one that refuses: a person whose work names a room that does not exist would simply never go to work, and you would spend an evening wondering why.

Two problems are advice rather than refusal. Somebody with no home, no work and no day will never go anywhere, and a world where nowhere is public gives nobody anywhere to spend an evening.

The smallest world that works

A name. At least one place, with one of them public and connected to the others, or nobody can walk anywhere. Everybody needs somewhere they live, somewhere they work, or a day, otherwise they stand still forever. And a player, if you want one.

Six to ten people, four to eight places, one or two unfinished situations is a good first world.

What a card cannot do yet

Being honest about this matters more than the feature list. A card world is a street with people and debts on it. Tremont is a city with a machine underneath it, and most of that machine is not yet expressible in a card. No secrets with trust thresholds. No ladders of wants. No rules for what gives a secret away. No conditions for what would count as a situation being over, since a card's situation is debt-shaped. No rooms inside buildings, and therefore no doors and no earshot. No weekday patterns in a schedule. No calendar, climate or voice. No phone apps, couples or signed terms.

Anyone whose Tuesday differs from their Sunday currently needs the code route. The gap is being closed deliberately rather than pretended away.

Writing one in code

The larger route is a file that declares the same things with more room: places that nest, schedules with priorities and weekday masks, secrets with the trust it takes to hear them, rules for what gives a thing away, ladders of wants, a calendar, a climate, and the voice the narrator writes in. Then one line to register it, and the command line and the app both open it the same way.

Three things are worth knowing about extending the engine itself.

You can declare your own kinds of situation. Seven ship, including debt, promise, grudge and rivalry, and they are ordinary declarations rather than privileged ones. A world with oaths, curses or apprenticeships declares those instead, and says which side counts as the wronged one when one breaks. The engine never learns what any of them mean.

You can declare what your world counts. Coin is one declared quantity among however many you want. A world that wants mana writes two declarations and no engine code, saying whether it is conserved, its floor, its ceiling, and whether it comes back on its own. Saying it is conserved is a strong claim, and the world then proves every turn that nobody minted any.

You can add verbs, mostly as data: what a verb requires and what it does. A whole magic system exists as a worked example, written entirely in world content with nothing patched in the engine, and by the time it reaches adjudication nothing can tell it apart from a built-in one.

A world a model wrote

You can describe a world in a sentence and have one generated, such as "a rain-soaked bus depot at the end of the line". The result is checked rather than trusted, the same way a narrator's tags are. A card naming a room that does not exist is handed back with that exact sentence and it tries again, three times, then gives up and prints what is still wrong instead of handing you a world that does not run. One call, about a penny.

Treat it as a first draft that is guaranteed to start, not as an author.
