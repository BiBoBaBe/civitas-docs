Why it works this way

None of this is a new idea. It is ordinary engineering, applied to a problem that is usually solved by asking a model to try harder.

Civitas was written by somebody who builds production software for a living, and the shape of it comes straight from that work: state is explicit; events are the source of truth; behaviour is derived from data rather than restated in prose. An append-only log you can replay. One place where truth lives, with views computed from it instead of copied out of it. Invariants that are checked rather than hoped for. Tests written from real failures rather than from imagined ones. Small composable operations with preconditions, which either apply or are refused.

Most roleplay tooling skips all of it and asks the model to be its own database. A model asked to do that drifts. It loses track of who was in the room. It has somebody give up the same secret twice. And it cannot separate what is true from what a particular person believes, because a transcript has nowhere to put the difference.

That difference is a worked example. In Civitas, a debt is a fact and knowing about it is a link from a person to that fact, carrying their version of it. Pay the debt and the fact changes; the woman across town who was never told still says you owe eighty, and she says it in good faith. Nothing had to remember to be inconsistent. It is inconsistent because two different things are being recorded.

The same thinking runs through the rest. A verb that tries to spend the player's money is not discouraged in a system prompt, it is refused, because the precondition says so. Fixing something recorded wrongly is a new event rather than an edit, so the history stays honest about having been corrected.

The result is that continuity is a property of the system instead of a thing you keep asking a model to do.
