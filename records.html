What the world records

A scene the world never heard about is a scene that did not happen.

Say you tip a bartender a hundred in front of a full room. Five things follow. A fact is written that you did it. Everybody standing there gets a firsthand belief in that fact, at full confidence. From that moment they can repeat it, and some of them will. If the amount was large enough to be an answer to a question nobody asked, it also tells the room something about you. And none of it happened because a model remembered to be consistent.

How a turn is read

Two passes read every reply. The narrator tags what it did, such as she moved here, this situation escalated, he paid her forty. A second pass reads the finished prose for what the narrator did not bother to say: what came off, what each person is doing, who had been drinking.

Then everything is checked. A tag that does not survive is rejected with a reason and a suggestion, never half-applied and never silently dropped. There is one more try, keeping the prose and fixing only the rejected tags, and then the turn stands.

The kinds of record

Deeds, meaning what you did
Something the room will remember: you tipped somebody a hundred, stood up for somebody, made a scene. You know what you did. What is worth recording is who else now does.
What you said about yourself
You told somebody something about your own life. Filed separately from a deed because it is hearsay about you rather than something witnessed. They may bring it up, repeat it, or hold it against you. It carries a volume, and by default it goes to the person in front of you rather than to the hall.
What people told you
Ordinary life details somebody shared, and separately, things they had been keeping. Both become permanently true of that person. Notice what this is not: it is never the world deciding what you believe. Somebody telling you a thing is their act, gated on their trust in you rather than on a narrator's sense that it is probably about time.
Plans
An arrangement made out loud, which the world then keeps. See Plans.
Texts
Messages actually sent, word for word, so the phone thread has them. A text is witnessed by the two ends and by nobody standing in the room, because people do not read over your shoulder. But reading a message in company is a thing other people see you do, so the room gets the moment and never the words.

The world decides who reaches for their phone

Unprompted texts are a good illustration of the general split. The world decides that somebody is thinking about you and why. The narrator writes the words.

A model asked "does anybody text him?" says yes every turn, and one never told that Jade is thinking about him never has her text at all.

Only people who have your number, who are not in the room with you, who are awake at a civil hour, and who have an actual reason: something from their day, warmth after a day apart, desire late at night. Each person at most once in twenty hours, and the whole street at most once every ninety minutes, so a phone is an occasional buzz and not a group chat.

A buzz nobody gets round to writing within six hours simply never happened. It appears on no phone and in no diary, because a checking-in text from six hours ago is not sent at three in the morning. It is just never sent.

What the narrator may never do

However the prose reads, the narrator cannot move you, spend your money, decide what you feel, decide what you believe, take something off you, or claim you said or agreed to something. Each is refused unless your own message warrants it.

The refusals are written like notes to a writer rather than error codes. "That is the player's to decide." "Tell him in the prose and let him decide." "They have to be face to face."

Situations, and why they are not just facts

Three things are easy to confuse, and the engine keeps them apart. A condition is a plain fact of the world: she owes him forty. A belief is what one person holds about that, and it does not update when the condition does. A situation is the thing that is unfinished, with stakes and sides, pulling people across the map towards each other.

The rule that makes it click: conditions are conditions, and situations are conditions somebody has decided to do something about. She can owe him forty for years and it means nothing. The situation opens the day he stops being patient. The fact did not change. The situation did.

So a debt can be paid, the situation closed, and half the district still believe she owes eighty, because news of the payment has to reach each of them separately. A debt settled quietly that silently corrected everybody's beliefs across town would be exactly backwards.

Situations also end by themselves when they have quietly become true. What counts as an ending is declared as a condition rather than a list of solutions.

Rent her the basement, give her the bar's back room, move her into the penthouse, or something neither of us thought of, and the same check notices. Enumerating routes is what makes a world feel like a menu.

The journal

What happened so far is a query over the log, not a summary written by a model. Every event carries a significance from zero to one. Background noise is dropped, the most significant dozen are kept, and then they are put back into chronological order, because a recap is a story and not a leaderboard.

It is filtered to what you actually witnessed and rendered with the names you had at the time, so a journal of last week does not use a name you only learned yesterday, or report a door you were not in the room to see. It is recomputed from the log each time, so it cannot drift, and it costs nothing.
