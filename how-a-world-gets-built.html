How a world gets built

Playing is a user interface. Authoring mostly is not.

Everything below exists and is used. Several steps are scripts run by hand, and this page says so each time rather than describing them as buttons. I would like them to be easier to reach. They are not today.

1. A premise becomes a cast

Writing the people is the hand-written part, and there is no tool for it. What you produce is a list: who they are, what they do for a living, where they live, who they know, and the part that makes a world rather than a set, which is what each of them wants and what it costs them, wired so the wants touch each other.

There is one piece of automation nearby. Describe a world in a sentence and a model will draft the roster and the map: six to ten people with a job, a home and a web of acquaintance. The result is then checked rather than trusted, and handed back with the exact problem if it names a room that does not exist. Three tries, then it gives up and prints what is still wrong rather than handing you a world that will not run.

It gives you the roster and the map. It does not give you the people.

2. Each person becomes a card that works on its own

A character card is the ordinary, portable kind: a description, a personality, a scenario, a greeting, example dialogue, tags. The card says who somebody is and how they talk. The world says where they are and what is happening. Where they disagree, the world wins, because the card may have been written for another story.

That drives the whole step. Cards taken from an existing story carry a premise, an address and a first message that all assert things the world is about to contradict, including assertions about who you already are to them. So the cards are rebuilt as person-only copies: keep the looks, the voice, the history and the example dialogue, and strip everything that belonged to the story they came from. Cards written fresh for a world keep a generic scenario and a greeting on purpose, so each one is still playable on its own, outside any world, as an ordinary character.

Cards also carry an optional block that the engine reads and ordinary apps ignore. Pronouns, because a narrator handed a role with no pronouns defaults to "he" every time. Secrets, meaning a thing kept and the trust it takes to say it. A ranked list given up one item at a time, where each item asks slightly more trust than the last, so the world can say how far along it you are rather than asking a model to keep count over a thousand turns. And a blank last line on such a list, whose words get written in play, because the world holds the slot and the gate rather than the sentence.

Cards are matched to people by name, deliberately, because an identifier written in two places is bookkeeping that puts people off. Matching ignores case and punctuation and nothing else. A card nobody answers to is reported, not attached to whoever was closest. A person with no card is voiced from the world.

Script, today. The cards are generated by scripts and posted to the character library by hand.

3. Each person gets a pinned look

A look is two things. A base of what never changes: build, skin tone, hair colour and style, eyes, glasses, anything always visible. And a small wardrobe by situation: home, work, a night out, casual, and the gym if they use one.

Which outfit a picture uses is decided from what they are doing and, importantly, where they are standing, because where outranks doing. That was a real failure. Somebody in a locked gym studio came out in a denim skirt, because the words for the moment had stopped saying gym the second the workout stopped.

Every look is checked before it can ship, and each check comes from a mistake. The first tag has to state an adult. Hair and eye colour belong to the base only, because two hair descriptions in one prompt gets you a ponytail and loose hair at the same time. Every garment needs a colour. A list past a certain length gets its tail quietly dropped by the image model.

Script, today. One table for the whole cast, edited by hand. Changing how somebody looks means rerunning the card script and reposting the card.

4. Each person gets one reference head

This is what makes the same face come back every time. Render a portrait from their pinned look, crop the head and shoulders, save it. At render time that one image steers the face and hair while the prompt still controls the outfit, the pose, the room and the framing.

Three things learned the hard way. Use a head crop rather than a whole portrait, because the technique copies whatever is in the crop, background and props included. Keep its influence in a narrow band, since turned up further it starts choosing the outfit and the room as well. And if the portrait and the look tags disagree you get a blend, because the technique cannot resolve a contradiction. Fix one or the other.

Somebody with no reference head still gets a picture. They keep their look tags and no face steering.

Script, today. Render, crop by hand, drop the file in, run the install script.

5. The people are seeded into a world

Then the world file: places that nest, who lives and works where, each person's timetable, who knows whom and how well, how they feel about specific other people, what each of them is keeping, what is unfinished and what would count as it being over.

Two routes, covered in full on making your own world. A data file anybody can write and trade, or code for the things the data format cannot yet say. Either way it ends as a list of events in a log, and the state is read off that log.

The world is then run forward from midnight before anybody plays in it, so the evening you walk into already has a day behind it.

6. Adding somebody to a world already being played

A session is its own log, so a person added to the seed after a session began is simply not in that world. The graft tool exists for that, and its restraint is the interesting part.

Rooms inside places the save already has are added automatically when a world is opened, along with the doors touching them. Nothing that already exists is ever changed, because everything that exists has been lived in. A whole new district is not added this way, because empty streets with nobody on them would be worse than no streets.

Adding a person is surgery, and the tool treats it that way. It is a dry run by default and prints the entire plan before it will write anything. The new person starts where their day has them at this hour, behind the bar and mid-shift, rather than where the seed had them at midnight. Their arrival is backdated half an hour, so nobody is announced as having just walked into a room they work in. They are given a night at home behind them, or the first thing they do is leave work to go and have one. Everything is written as authored and with no significance, so none of it turns up in anybody's journal as an event.

There is also a mode that brings people already in the save up to date with fields the seed has since grown. It only fills what is missing. A field with a different value is listed and never applied, because it has been lived in, and it will never touch a name, an identity or a card binding.

A driver was added to a live save mid-story this way, with the story running, and nothing restarted.

Script, today. A command with a save file, a dry run, and a warning to back the file up and not to run it while the world is live.

7. Repairing something recorded wrongly

Scene reading is not perfect. A throwaway line gets read as a much larger confession. A duplicate situation gets opened twice. A state change gets recorded an hour after it started.

The answer is not to edit the history. It is a small set of repair operations that are adjudicated exactly like anything else, so a bad repair is refused too. Take back a telling that never happened, and only a telling can be taken back: it refuses unless the person actually holds that belief and came by it by being told. Fold a duplicate situation into the one that stands. Backdate a state change to when it really started.

A correction is an event, not an edit, so the log stays honest about having been corrected.

There is also a proper undo that is not a repair at all. Deleting a reply rewinds the world to before it, forks, and replays everything after it on the new timeline.

Script, today. Repairs are issued as a request, not through a screen.

The point of listing it plainly is that none of it is magic and all of it is repeatable. The parts that are scripts are scripts because they were built in the order the work needed them, not because they have to stay that way.
